Photograph the result, not just the numbers

        

          
    
            
  • A photo settles arguments that numbers can't. "Acceptable char" means one thing to you and another to a first-time user.
    • 
            
  • Shoot the back face for cut recipes. That is where char, taper and incomplete release actually show.
    • 
            
  • Include a scale reference — a coin or a steel rule — so detail size reads correctly later.
    • 
            
  • Same light every time. A phone on the bed under the lid light is consistent enough.
    • 
            
  • Photos are resized to about 1000 px on save, so a full library stays small enough to export in one file.

Reading the card

        

          
    
            
  • Cut card: the winner is the fastest cell that releases cleanly when you push it out, with the least char on the back face.
    • 
            
  • Engrave card: judge under the same light you'll photograph the part in. Contrast, not depth, is usually what people notice.
    • 
            
  • Write the winning cell on the offcut with the date and sheet supplier. Sheet-to-sheet variation is bigger than most people expect, especially plywood.
    • 
            
  • Re-run the card after a lens clean, mirror alignment, or a new tube — a 60 W tube at end of life behaves like a 40 W tube.

Never put these in the laser

    

      
Chlorinated and fluorinated plastics release acid gas that destroys the machine and injures people. If a material's composition is unknown and unlabelled, it does not go in the laser.

The model, in plain terms

    

      
    
        
  • Cutting is line energy. What gets a beam through a sheet is joules delivered per millimetre of travel: power in watts divided by speed in mm/s. Doubling power and doubling speed lands in roughly the same place. The app estimates the line energy each material and thickness needs, then solves for speed at a sensible power level.
    • 
        
  • Thickness costs more than linearly. Deeper kerfs lose energy to the walls and to debris, so required energy scales at about thickness1.15, not thickness. That's why 6 mm is more than twice the work of 3 mm.
    • 
        
  • Engraving is area energy. Raster work is joules per square millimetre: power divided by (speed × line interval). Line interval comes from your lines-per-inch, so raising LPI at fixed speed and power deepens and darkens the result.
    • 
        
  • Extra passes are not free. Each additional pass is modelled at about 85% of the previous one's usefulness, because the beam is now focused above the fresh cut face and debris is in the way. Two passes at high speed usually beat one pass at a crawl for char, but cost time and edge squareness.
    • 
        
  • Wavelength decides what's possible at all. A CO₂ beam is absorbed by organics and acrylic and reflected by bare metal. A 445 nm diode is absorbed by dark material and passes straight through clear acrylic. A 1064 nm fiber beam marks metal and mostly ignores wood. The app blocks combinations that physically can't work rather than giving you a number that will fail.
    • 
        
  • Speed units are one setting, converted exactly. The engine works in mm/s because that is what the energy maths needs, but every input and every displayed number passes through the unit switch in the header. mm/min is the default: it is what GRBL firmware, Creality's spec sheets and Falcon Design Space all speak, so nobody has to multiply by sixty in their head at the machine. Flip it to mm/s if you are cross-referencing a CO₂ or Ruida settings table, which is where mm/s is the convention.
    • 
        
  • A saved recipe outranks the model. Anything in the Recipes tab was proven on the actual machine, on actual stock, by someone who looked at the result. When a recipe exists for the material and machine you have selected, it appears above the estimate. Trust it first.
    • 
        
  • Tube age is the hidden variable. A rated wattage is a nameplate, not a measurement. Calibration is the fix: give the app one setting you know works and it re-derives that material's constant for your actual machine and optics.
    • 
      

    

  

  

    
What the confidence badge means

    

      
    
        
  • Modelled — derived from published-typical energy values. Treat as a starting point and expect to adjust by 10–25%.
    • 
        
  • Calibrated — anchored to a setting you verified on this machine. Much tighter, especially near the thickness you calibrated at.
    • 
        
  • Out of range — the request is past what this source and wattage can reasonably do. The suggestion shown is the best available, not a promise.
